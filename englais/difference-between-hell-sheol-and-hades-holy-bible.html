📚 The Difference Between Hell, Sheol and Hades - Holy Bible

What each term means in the Holy Bible · Bible Word God

Sheol and Hades

Sheol is the Hebrew word of the Old Testament for the abode of the dead or the grave. Jacob said he would go down to Sheol mourning his son (Genesis 37:35). Hades is its Greek equivalent: Peter quotes Psalm 16 and says that God did not abandon the soul of Christ in Hades (Acts 2:27).

Gehenna and the lake of fire

Gehenna is the term Jesus uses for the final judgment (Matthew 5:29-30). It comes from the Valley of Hinnom, near Jerusalem. The lake of fire appears in Revelation 20:14, where death and Hades are thrown into it: this is the final destination.

Why it matters to distinguish them

Many translations use the word "hell" for several of these terms, which creates confusion. When reading, it is wise to ask which original word is being used and in what context. But there is one truth that does not change: Christ has "the keys of hell and of death" (Revelation 1:18). Whoever trusts in him has nothing to fear.

Sheol in the Old Testament

The word Sheol appears more than sixty times in the Old Testament. Sometimes it simply means the grave, the destiny of all human beings. Jacob said he would go down "I will go down into the grave unto my son mourning" (Genesis 37:35). In other passages it expresses the idea of a dark place where communion with the living is lost, as when the psalmist says that in death there is no remembrance of God (Psalm 6:5).

But the Old Testament also shows a growing hope. The psalmist says: "For thou wilt not leave my soul in hell" (Psalm 16:10) and "God will redeem my soul from the power of the grave" (Psalm 49:15). This hope is fully realized in the resurrection of Christ.

Hades in the New Testament

Hades is the Greek word translators used for Sheol. Jesus told Peter that the gates of Hades would not prevail against his Church (Matthew 16:18). In Revelation 6:8, Hades follows death as its companion. In Revelation 20:13-14, the dead are given up by Hades, and then death and Hades are thrown into the lake of fire.

This means that Hades is temporary. It is not the final destination, but a transitional state that will disappear when God makes all things new.

Gehenna and Tartarus

Gehenna appears about a dozen times in the New Testament, almost always on the lips of Jesus. It was the Valley of Hinnom, south of Jerusalem, a place associated with idolatry and later with garbage and fire. Jesus used it as an image of final judgment.

Tartarus appears only once, in 2 Peter 2:4, and refers to the place where God keeps the angels who sinned until the judgment. It should not be confused with the destination of human beings.

What we learn by comparing

By studying these terms, we see that the Bible speaks of a process: death, an intermediate state and the final judgment. Some older translations render all these terms as "hell," and that is why there is confusion. Modern translations generally keep Sheol, Hades and Gehenna.

Here is the essential point for everyday life: Christ has conquered death and has authority over all these places. Whoever trusts in him need not fear. Paul rejoices in it: "O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?" (1 Corinthians 15:55). Take time to read these passages calmly, with a study Bible.

Frequently asked questions

Which Bible should I use to study these terms? A study edition with notes, or a modern translation that indicates the original word. That way you will know when the text says Sheol, Hades or Gehenna.

Should I worry about understanding every detail? No. The essential thing is to trust in Christ, who has conquered death. Study with patience, and leave in God's hands what remains unclear, as Deuteronomy 29:29 says.

To reflect on this week

The study of words is useful when it leads us to Christ. Note each time you see a term and the context where it appears.

Suggested verses: Psalm 16:10, Acts 2:27, Matthew 5:29, Revelation 1:18 and 1 Corinthians 15:55. Read one each day, ask yourself what it teaches you about God and what concrete step you can take. Finish with a short prayer, giving thanks for what you have learned and asking for help to put it into practice. Sharing what you discover with a loved one or a friend helps you remember it and may encourage others.
