📖 Book of Exodus

God sets his people free · 40 chapters

Background

Tradition attributes Exodus to Moses, who lived the events he recounts. Many place the departure from Egypt in the 15th century BC, although others propose the 13th century. The book is addressed to the descendants of Jacob, enslaved by the Egyptians, and tells how God delivered them, led them to Sinai, and dwelt among them through the tabernacle.

Central message

Exodus reveals the God who hears the cry of the oppressed, remembers his covenant, and acts with power to save. Israel is freed by the blood of the lamb and the parted waters, then shaped into a holy nation by the law. The book ends with the glory of the Lord filling the tabernacle, showing that the goal of redemption is not only freedom, but to live near God.

Structure of the book

  • Chapters 1 to 4: the oppression in Egypt, the birth of Moses, and his call before the bush that burned without being consumed.
  • Chapters 5 to 12: the confrontation with Pharaoh, the ten plagues, and the first Passover with the sacrificed lamb.
  • Chapters 13 to 18: the departure, the crossing of the Red Sea, the manna, the water from the rock, and the victory over Amalek.
  • Chapters 19 to 24: the arrival at Sinai, the Ten Commandments, the laws for the people, and the confirmation of the covenant.
  • Chapters 25 to 40: the instructions for the tabernacle, the golden calf, Moses' intercession, and the glory that fills the dwelling.

Main themes

The first theme is liberation. God presents himself as the one who sees affliction, hears the weeping, and comes down to deliver. No power, not even Pharaoh, can stop his purpose. This salvation is free and begins with divine initiative. Christians recognize in it a shadow of the redemption that Christ accomplished for those who were slaves to sin.

The second theme is the Passover and the covenant. The blood of the lamb on the doorposts spared Israel from judgment, and then the Lord made a covenant with the people at Sinai. The commandments were not given to earn salvation, but to teach a people already delivered how to live. The New Testament calls Christ our Passover, sacrificed for us.

The third theme is the presence of God. The Lord does not merely bring his people out of Egypt; he wants to dwell among them. The tabernacle, with its ark and its sanctuary, teaches that God is holy and that approaching him requires a divine provision. The sin of the golden calf shows human frailty, while Moses' intercession foreshadows a better mediator.

Key verses

  • Exodus 3:14
  • Exodus 12:13
  • Exodus 20:2-3
  • Exodus 34:6-7

Reflecting on the verses

In Exodus 3:14, God reveals his name as the one who is and always will be. Exodus 12:13 promises that when he sees the blood, the Lord will pass over. In Exodus 20:2-3, the commandments begin by recalling that he brought them out of slavery and that he therefore deserves to be the only God. And Exodus 34:6-7 describes his character: compassionate, slow to anger, abounding in kindness, yet just.

Application for today

Exodus reminds us that the Christian life begins with grace: we were delivered before we obeyed. Today, many things claim to enslave us, such as fear, vices, or pride, and God still sets people free. It also invites us to appreciate worship and the Lord's presence in our everyday lives, and to cultivate a grateful heart that obeys out of love and not out of fear. May each morning we remember where the Lord has brought us from and where he is leading us.

Questions for reflection

  • What slavery do I need to hand over to God so that he can set me free?
  • How does the work of Christ, our Passover, change the way I obey?
  • How can I make more room for the Lord's presence in my everyday life?

Prayer

God of liberation, thank you for hearing my cry and delivering me by the blood of Christ. Teach me to walk with you in obedience, to worship you with reverence, and to live near your presence. Amen.
