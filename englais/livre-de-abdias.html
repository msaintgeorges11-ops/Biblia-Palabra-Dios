📖 Book of Obadiah

The pride of Edom collapses · 1 chapter

Background

Obadiah, whose name means servant of the Lord, wrote the shortest book of the Old Testament. His lineage is unknown, and the date is debated: many place it after the destruction of Jerusalem by Babylon in 586 BC. Edom, descended from Esau, Jacob's brother, lived in the rocky mountains south of the Dead Sea. It rejoiced when Jerusalem fell and plundered its brother people.

Central message

The central message is that God judges pride and violence against his people, and that no one can hide from him, however high their fortress. Edom trusted in its rocks and its allies, but it will be humbled for what it did to its brother Jacob. We reap what we sow. In the end, the kingdom will belong to the Lord, and the saved will go up on Mount Zion.

Structure of the book

  • Verses 1 to 9: the announcement of judgment against Edom, whose pride and fortress will not be able to save it.
  • Verses 10 to 14: the reasons for the punishment: violence against his brother Jacob and gloating over his misfortune.
  • Verses 15 and 16: the day of the Lord upon all nations and the principle that as you have done, it will be done to you.
  • Verses 17 and 18: deliverance on Mount Zion and the house of Jacob that will be like a fire.
  • Verses 19 to 21: the restoration of the lands of Israel and the final declaration that the kingdom will be the Lord's.

Main themes

The first theme is the danger of pride. Edom said in its heart that no one would bring it down, for it lived in the clefts of the rock. God answers that even if it soars like the eagle, from there he will bring it down. Security founded on wealth, power, or position is an illusion. Only the Lord is a true refuge, and he humbles the proud.

The second theme is the offense of rejoicing at another's downfall. Edom did not merely neglect to help its brother, but stationed itself at the crossroads to cut down those who were fleeing. The book condemns indifference, exploitation, and mockery in the face of another's pain. God expects compassion, especially among those who share ties of blood or faith.

The third theme is God's just government. The day of the Lord will come upon all nations, and what they have done will be done to them. This judgment comforts the oppressed, for no injustice will go unanswered. And in the end, the deliverers will go up to judge, and the kingdom will belong to the Lord, a hope that is fully realized in Christ.

Key verses

  • Obadiah 1:3-4
  • Obadiah 1:12
  • Obadiah 1:15
  • Obadiah 1:21

Reflecting on the verses

In Obadiah 1:3-4, God exposes the deception of the proud heart, which thinks itself too high to fall. Obadiah 1:12 warns against looking with pleasure on the day of a brother's misfortune. In Obadiah 1:15, the rule of divine justice is stated: as you have done, it will be done to you. And Obadiah 1:21 ends by proclaiming that the kingdom will belong to the Lord.

Application for today

Obadiah invites us to guard our hearts. When someone who has hurt us stumbles, do we feel joy or compassion? It also asks us not to boast of what we have, because we have received everything. And it encourages us not to despair in the face of injustice, because the Lord reigns. We can leave vengeance to him, pray for those who do us wrong, and help the one who is in trouble. Every day is an opportunity to choose humility, to speak well of others, and to remember that we too need God's mercy.

Questions for reflection

  • In what do I feel so secure that I might forget to depend on God?
  • Have I felt satisfaction at the failure of someone I have had conflicts with?
  • How can I help a relative or a brother or sister who is going through a hard time this week?

Prayer

Lord, you humble the proud and lift up the lowly. Keep my heart from pride and mockery, give me compassion for those who suffer, and help me to wait patiently for your just reign. Amen.
