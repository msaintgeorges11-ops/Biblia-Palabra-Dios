📖 Book of Amos

Let justice roll down like waters · 9 chapters

Background

Amos was a shepherd and a farmer of sycamore figs from Tekoa, in Judah, and did not belong to any school of prophets. God sent him to prophesy to the northern kingdom, Israel, in the time of Uzziah of Judah and Jeroboam II of Israel, around the middle of the 8th century BC, two years before an earthquake. It was a time of wealth and comfort for a few, while the poor were trampled and sold for debts.

Central message

The central message is that God demands justice and does not tolerate a religion that coexists with oppression. Amos denounces the neighboring nations, then Judah and Israel, because the covenant people have greater responsibility. Worship, feasts, and songs are offensive to him when the just treatment of one's neighbor is lacking. But the book ends in hope: God will raise up the fallen booth of David.

Structure of the book

  • Chapters 1 and 2: the judgments on the neighboring nations, on Judah, and on Israel for their repeated sins.
  • Chapters 3 and 4: the word against Samaria, the wealthy women, and the people's refusal to return to the Lord.
  • Chapters 5 and 6: a lament over Israel, the call to seek God and to let justice flow.
  • Chapters 7 and 8: the visions of the locusts, the fire, the plumb line, and the basket of ripe fruit.
  • Chapter 9: the vision of the Lord beside the altar, the inevitable judgment, and the promised restoration of David.

Main themes

The first theme is social justice. Amos points a finger at those who sell the righteous for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals, and at those who cheat with false weights. God sees every abuse and does not pass over it in silence. True faith is shown in how we treat the weak, the day laborer, and the foreigner, and in defending what is right even when it costs us.

The second theme is empty religion. God says he hates their feasts and will not accept their offerings, because worship was separated from daily life. He does not reject worship itself, but hypocrisy. A church full of songs but blind to the pain of others does not please the Lord. What he asks for is a people who worship and practice justice.

The third theme is the responsibility of the one who has received more. Only Israel had been known by the Lord among all the families of the earth, and therefore he would punish it for its iniquities. Privilege carries a duty. Yet God promises to raise up David's fallen booth and to restore his people, a hope that in the New Testament is fulfilled in Christ and in the calling of the nations.

Key verses

  • Amos 3:2
  • Amos 4:12
  • Amos 5:24
  • Amos 9:11

Reflecting on the verses

In Amos 3:2, God reminds us that he has known only Israel, and therefore he will hold it accountable. Amos 4:12 is a solemn warning: prepare to meet your God. In Amos 5:24, he asks that justice and righteousness flow like a never-failing stream. And Amos 9:11 announces that he will raise up the fallen booth of David and repair its breaches.

Application for today

Amos disturbs us in the best sense of the word. We can ask ourselves how we spend our money, how we treat those who work for us, and whether our voice defends those who have none. Sincere worship extends into acts of kindness and honesty. Whoever has received much from God must give much, and we can do so joyfully because he restores broken hearts.

Questions for reflection

  • Is there an injustice, near or far, in the face of which I have remained silent?
  • Does my Sunday worship show in my conduct during the week?
  • What privileges have I received that oblige me to serve others more?

Prayer

Righteous Lord, teach me to love what you love. May my worship go hand in hand with honesty and kindness toward the needy. Let your justice flow in my life and restore what has fallen. Amen.
