📖 Book of Isaiah

The salvation of the Suffering Servant · 66 chapters

Background

Isaiah, son of Amoz, prophesied in Jerusalem during the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, in the 8th century BC, while Assyria threatened Judah. Tradition attributes the whole book to him, although some scholars distinguish later sections. His audience was an unfaithful people who trusted in political alliances, and who needed to hear both the warning of judgment and the consolation of salvation.

Central message

The central message is that God is holy, judges sin, and at the same time saves his people by his grace. Isaiah announces punishment for idolatry and injustice, but also the coming of a King born of a virgin, of a Servant who would bear our transgressions, and of a new creation. The New Testament often cites this book to present Jesus as the promised Messiah.

Structure of the book

  • Chapters 1 to 12: the vision of judgment on Judah, Isaiah's call, and the promises of Immanuel.
  • Chapters 13 to 27: the messages of judgment on Babylon and other nations, and God's final hope.
  • Chapters 28 to 39: the warnings against trusting in Egypt, Hezekiah's deliverance, and his illness.
  • Chapters 40 to 55: the consolation of the people, the greatness of God, and the songs of the Servant of the Lord.
  • Chapters 56 to 66: the call to justice, the future glory of Zion, and the new heavens and the new earth.

Main themes

The first theme is the holiness and sovereignty of God. In his vision in the temple, Isaiah hears the seraphim singing that the Lord is holy, holy, holy. He recognizes himself as unclean, but he is forgiven and sent. God rules over the empires, and no idol compares to him. This greatness encourages trust and demands sincere worship, accompanied by justice toward our neighbor.

The second theme is the promised Messiah. Isaiah announces a child who will be called Immanuel, a son bearing the titles of Mighty God and Prince of Peace, and a shoot from the stump of Jesse. He also presents the Servant of the Lord, despised and wounded for our transgressions. By his wounds we are healed. This prophecy finds its fulfillment at the cross of Christ.

The third theme is consolation and hope for the people. After judgment, God promises to forgive, to restore, and to renew. Those who hope in the Lord find new strength. The book culminates in a vision of new heavens and a new earth, where there will be no more weeping. God will accomplish his plan, and his Word does not return to him empty.

Key verses

  • Isaiah 7:14
  • Isaiah 9:6
  • Isaiah 40:31
  • Isaiah 53:5

Reflecting on the verses

In 7:14, a sign is promised: a virgin will conceive and bear a son called Immanuel, God with us. In 9:6, the birth of a child who will reign with wonderful titles is announced. In 40:31, it is affirmed that those who hope in the Lord renew their strength and do not grow weary. And in 53:5, the Servant is described as wounded for our transgressions, by whose wounds we are healed. Each text points to Christ and to the God who fulfills his word.

Application for today

Isaiah invites us to look at God with reverence and to trust in him when the world seems unstable. We can examine whether our worship is reflected in justice and compassion. And when we feel tired, we can hope in the Lord, remembering that the suffering Servant has already borne our sins and that his victory is certain. May this hope move us to proclaim the Gospel with courage.

Questions for reflection

  • In what do I trust more than in the Lord when I face threats?
  • How is my worship reflected in the way I treat the poor and the needy?
  • What does it mean to me that Christ bore my sins?

Prayer

Holy, holy, holy, Lord God Almighty, forgive my unclean lips and heart. Thank you for Jesus, the Servant who bore my transgressions. Renew my strength and send me to serve you. Here I am. Amen.
