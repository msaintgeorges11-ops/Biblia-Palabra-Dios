📖 Book of Micah

Doing justice and loving mercy · 7 chapters

Background

Micah was from Moresheth, a village of Judah, and prophesied in the time of the kings Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, in the second half of the 8th century BC, a contemporary of Isaiah. He announced the fall of Samaria and the threat to Jerusalem. His message is addressed to a society where the powerful stripped people of fields and houses, where judges sold themselves, where there were false prophets and worship without obedience.

Central message

The central message combines judgment and hope. God accuses Israel and Judah of injustice, the corruption of their leaders, and idolatry, and announces punishment. But he promises to gather a remnant and to raise up a shepherd from little Bethlehem, whose rule will bring peace. What the Lord asks is simple and demanding: to do justice, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with him, and he will forgive the sins of his people.

Structure of the book

  • Chapters 1 and 2: the judgment on Samaria and Judah, and the denunciation of those who covet fields and houses.
  • Chapters 3 to 5: the condemnation of the leaders and the false prophets, the promise of the mountain of the Lord and of the ruler born in Bethlehem.
  • Chapter 6:1 to 8: God's lawsuit with his people and the answer about what he really asks.
  • Chapters 6:9 to 7:6: the accusations of fraud, violence, and corruption throughout society.
  • Chapter 7:7 to 20: the prophet's hope in God, and the praise of the God who forgives and casts sins into the depths of the sea.

Main themes

The first theme is the justice that God demands. Micah denounces those who plot evil on their beds and carry it out at the light of morning, and the rulers who devour the people. Faith without justice is hollow. The Lord cannot be bought with thousands of rams or with rivers of oil: he wants a life that is honest, compassionate, and humble before him and before others.

The second theme is the King who comes from Bethlehem. Although Bethlehem was small, from it would come the one who would rule over Israel and whose origin goes back to eternity. Herod's scholars cited this prophecy to find Jesus. God fulfills his promise to shepherd his people with power, and his reign will bring security and peace to the ends of the earth.

The third theme is the God who forgives. After so much judgment, Micah asks what God is like him, who pardons iniquity and delights in mercy. He will again have compassion, he will tread our iniquities underfoot and cast our sins into the depths of the sea. The believer's hope does not rest on their merits, but on God's faithfulness to his promises.

Key verses

  • Micah 4:3
  • Micah 5:2
  • Micah 6:8
  • Micah 7:18-19

Reflecting on the verses

In Micah 4:3, a time is announced when swords will be turned into plowshares and people will no longer learn war. Micah 5:2 points to Bethlehem as the birthplace of the eternal ruler. Micah 6:8 sums up what God asks: justice, mercy, and humility. And Micah 7:18-19 praises the God who forgives, does not stay angry, and casts our sins into the depths of the sea.

Application for today

Micah asks of us a faith that shows in daily life: in clean business dealings, truthful words, and compassion toward those who suffer. It also encourages us not to despair in times of corruption, because the promised King has already come and will return. And when sin weighs on us, we can come with confidence to the God who forgives, and share that same mercy with others.

Questions for reflection

  • In what area of my work or my home do I need to act with more justice?
  • What does it mean to me to walk humbly with my God every day?
  • How does it comfort me to know that God casts my confessed sins into the depths of the sea?

Prayer

Lord, teach me to do justice, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with you. Thank you for the King born in Bethlehem and for the forgiveness of my sins. Make me an instrument of your peace. Amen.
